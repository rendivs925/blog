## The Biefeld‑Brown Effect and Static High Voltage Limitations

The first attempt to use electricity for lift without moving parts was the Biefeld‑Brown effect, discovered in the 1920s. An asymmetric capacitor with a small electrode on top and a large electrode on bottom, when charged with high voltage DC, would move toward the smaller electrode. Mainstream physics explains this as ion wind in air, but experiments in hard vacuum have shown a residual force, suggesting that the electric field directly polarizes the quantum vacuum, creating a pressure gradient. This is called asymmetric electrostatic pressure, and it is a real effect.

However, static high voltage has severe limitations. The force is very small because a static field only aligns the virtual dipoles in the vacuum without creating a flow. It fights against the vacuum's restoring forces, much like pushing against a spring. To get any significant lift, you need millions of volts, which is dangerous, heavy, and inefficient. Most of the energy is lost to corona discharge, dielectric heating, and electromagnetic interference. Static high voltage is a brute force approach, and it is not the elegant solution we need.
